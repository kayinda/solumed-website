When a child starts coughing or has a fever, many families in Uganda reach for antibiotics before they reach a health worker. In a study of children under five with fever at a referral hospital in northern Uganda, four in ten had already been given antibiotics before they arrived. Most of these were bought at drug shops or given at clinics, and some came from leftover tablets at home or from a neighbour [1]. Caregivers who believed that fever, cough or diarrhoea always need antibiotics were more likely to give them.

That belief is understandable, but it is mostly wrong. Here is what the evidence says about the illnesses that send most of us looking for antibiotics.

## The common cold

Colds are caused by viruses. Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses. When researchers pooled the trials of antibiotics for the common cold, people who took antibiotics did no better than those who took a dummy tablet. Adults who took antibiotics were more than twice as likely to have side effects [2]. The same review found no benefit from antibiotics for a runny nose with thick yellow or green mucus, a sign many people take to mean a bacterial infection [2].

A cold usually lasts about a week. Rest, fluids, and paracetamol for fever or aches are what help.

## A cough that will not go away

Most coughs in otherwise healthy adults are acute bronchitis, an inflammation of the airways usually caused by a virus. The cough can last two or three weeks, which is why people often decide it needs an antibiotic. In trials, antibiotics shortened the cough by less than half a day on average, and people who took them were more likely to have side effects [3]. For most people, the small gain is not worth the cost, the side effects or the resistance it breeds.

A cough is different when it comes with fast or difficult breathing, chest pain, high fever, or blood in the sputum, or when it has lasted more than two weeks. Those need a health worker, because they can mean pneumonia or tuberculosis.

## Sore throat

Most sore throats are caused by viruses and settle on their own: in the trials, 82% of people who did not get antibiotics were free of symptoms within a week [4]. But some sore throats are caused by streptococcus bacteria. These matter more in Africa than in many richer countries, because untreated streptococcal throat infection can lead to rheumatic fever, which can damage the heart valves for life. Antibiotics reduce the risk of rheumatic fever after a sore throat [4].

So a sore throat with fever, swollen tender glands in the neck, pus on the tonsils and no cough, especially in a child or teenager, should be seen by a health worker, who can decide whether penicillin is needed. Do not treat it with leftover tablets.

## Fever in children

A child with fever should be tested for malaria. When the test is negative, it is tempting to give an antibiotic just in case. In studies across six countries, including Uganda, patients who tested negative for malaria were more likely to receive antibiotics than those who tested positive [5]. A negative malaria test does not mean the child has a bacterial infection. Many fevers in children are viral and pass in a few days.

Better tests help. When community health workers in rural Uganda used a finger-prick test called CRP, which shows how much inflammation is in the body, to decide which children with fever and cough needed antibiotics, antibiotic use fell from 92% to 71%, and the children did no worse [6]. In six Ugandan hospitals, training and guidelines doubled the share of patients with upper respiratory infections who were treated without antibiotics, from 26% to 53% [7].

## When to go to a health facility now

Antibiotics save lives when the infection is bacterial and serious. Go to a health facility straight away if:

- a child is breathing fast, or the lower chest pulls in when breathing in;
- a child cannot drink or breastfeed, vomits everything, is very sleepy or hard to wake, or has a convulsion [8];
- an adult is short of breath, confused, or has chest pain with fever;
- a cough has lasted more than two weeks, or there is blood in the sputum or weight loss;
- a fever lasts more than two or three days, or keeps coming back;
- anyone with HIV, diabetes or another long-term illness, a newborn, a pregnant woman or an older person develops a fever or a bad cough.

## What to do instead of reaching for antibiotics

1. **Get assessed before buying medicine.** At a drug shop, ask for advice rather than a named antibiotic.
2. **Test for malaria when there is fever.** If the test is negative, ask what else could cause the fever rather than asking for an antibiotic.
3. **Treat the symptoms.** Fluids, rest, paracetamol for fever or pain, warm drinks and honey for a cough in anyone over one year old.
4. **Ask your health worker two questions.** Is this a bacterial infection? What should I watch for, and when should I come back?
5. **If you are given an antibiotic, take it as prescribed.** Never keep leftovers, and never give your antibiotics to someone else.

Using antibiotics only when they are needed keeps them working for the times they really matter. You can read more about why this matters in our article on [why antibiotics are failing in Uganda](why-antibiotics-are-failing-in-uganda.html).
