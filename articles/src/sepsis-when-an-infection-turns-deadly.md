Few people in Uganda use the word sepsis, yet it sits behind many deaths that families describe as "a fever that got worse" or "an infection that spread". Worldwide, about one in five deaths is related to sepsis, and the burden is highest in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Knowing the warning signs can save a life.

## What is sepsis?

Sepsis happens when the body's response to an infection goes out of control and starts to damage its own tissues and organs [2,3]. It can lead to shock, failure of organs such as the kidneys and lungs, and death, especially when it is not recognised and treated early [2].

Sepsis always starts with an infection, often an ordinary one: pneumonia, a urine infection, an infected wound, an infection after childbirth or surgery, or an infection in the belly. Bacteria are the usual cause, but viruses, parasites and fungi can also lead to sepsis [2]. In a study of Ugandan adults admitted with sepsis, most of whom were living with HIV, cytomegalovirus, tuberculosis and malaria parasites were among the infections most often found in the blood [4].

## How big is the problem?

In 2017 there were an estimated 48.9 million cases of sepsis and 11 million sepsis-related deaths worldwide [1]. Almost half of all cases were in children under five [2].

Ugandan hospitals see the same picture. In hospitals in Uganda and Rwanda, 30% of adults admitted with sepsis died [5]. The danger does not end at discharge. In a Ugandan study that followed adults with sepsis for a year, almost half of the deaths happened after the first month [6]. Among young children discharged from Ugandan hospitals after suspected sepsis, about 1 in 17 of those not exposed to HIV died within six months, and about 1 in 8 of those living with HIV [7].

## Who is most at risk?

Anyone with an infection can develop sepsis, but the risk is higher for [2]:

- newborn babies and young children
- older people
- women who are pregnant or have recently given birth
- people with a weak immune system, for example from HIV or cancer
- people with long-term illnesses such as kidney or liver disease
- patients already in hospital, especially in intensive care

## The warning signs

Sepsis is a medical emergency, and its signs can change over time. In adults, watch for [2]:

- fever, or a low temperature with shivering
- confusion
- difficulty breathing
- clammy, sweaty skin
- extreme pain or discomfort
- a fast heartbeat or a weak pulse
- passing little or no urine

In children, watch for fast breathing, convulsions, pale skin, unusual sleepiness or difficulty waking, and feeling cold to the touch. Children under five may also stop feeding, vomit repeatedly or stop passing urine [2].

If someone with an infection is getting worse instead of better, do not wait. Go to a hospital or health centre straight away, and say that you are worried about sepsis.

## Why every hour counts

Sepsis treatment depends on antibiotics, fluids and other care given early. In a study of more than 49,000 patients with sepsis in New York, each hour of delay before antibiotics were given was linked to about 4% higher odds of dying [8]. Time lost at home, at a drug shop or in a queue matters.

Delays happen inside hospitals too. Patients, caregivers and health workers in Uganda and Malawi described long queues, too few staff and missed triage as reasons care for sepsis was slow [9]. Health facilities can do a lot with simple steps: checking breathing rate, alertness and blood pressure at triage, and giving antibiotics quickly to patients who look septic.

Drug resistance makes sepsis harder to treat. Patients whose sepsis is caused by resistant bacteria are more likely to die in hospital [2]. That is one more reason to use antibiotics carefully, as we explain in [why antibiotics are failing in Uganda](why-antibiotics-are-failing-in-uganda.html).

## For health workers

Under the international definition, adults with a suspected infection are more likely to have sepsis with a poor outcome if they have at least two of the following: a breathing rate of 22 per minute or more, any change in mental state, or a systolic blood pressure of 100 mmHg or less [3]. This check, called qSOFA, needs no laboratory and takes a minute. Where blood culture is available, take a sample before the first antibiotic dose, but do not let it delay treatment.

## After leaving hospital

Recovery from sepsis can take weeks, and people remain at risk after they go home [6,7]. Keep follow-up appointments, finish any treatment as prescribed, eat well, and go back early if fever, fast breathing or weakness returns. In Uganda, a programme that used phone and text reminders after children left hospital helped caregivers understand post-discharge care, and the reminders made follow-up easier [10].

## Preventing sepsis

The best way to prevent sepsis is to prevent infections and treat them early [2]:

- wash hands and prepare food safely
- use safe water and clean toilets
- make sure children receive all their routine vaccines
- breastfeed newborns
- get infections such as urine, chest or wound infections checked early, rather than waiting or self-treating

## The bottom line

Sepsis can follow almost any infection, and it kills quickly. Learn the signs, act fast when someone with an infection is getting worse, and ask the health worker directly: could this be sepsis?
