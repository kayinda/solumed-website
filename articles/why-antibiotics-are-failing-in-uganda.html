Why antibiotics are failing in Uganda, and what you can do about it

By Kayinda Francis · 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

Antibiotic resistance is already making common infections harder to treat in Uganda. A plain-language guide to what it is, why it is happening here, and the simple things that help.

Most of us have taken antibiotics at some point. Many of us have also bought them at a drug shop without seeing a health worker, taken a few tablets until we felt better, and kept the rest for next time. For years that seemed harmless, but the cost is now showing in our hospitals.

What is antibiotic resistance?

Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them from growing. Each time bacteria meet an antibiotic, most of them die. A few may survive because of small changes in their genes. Those survivors multiply and pass the change on, sometimes even to other kinds of bacteria. Over time the antibiotic stops working against them.

The bacteria become resistant, not the person. That is why resistance spreads: resistant bacteria pass from person to person, through hospitals, through food and water, and between animals and people.

Antibiotic resistance is part of a wider problem called antimicrobial resistance, or AMR, which also covers medicines against viruses, parasites and fungi. This article is about bacteria.

How big is the problem?

Worldwide, resistant bacterial infections directly caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019 and played a part in 4.95 million. Death rates were highest in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Without action, deaths caused directly by resistance are forecast to reach 1.91 million a year by 2050 [2].

Uganda's own data point the same way. Ceftriaxone is one of the antibiotics used most in our hospitals for serious infections [3]. In national surveillance of bacteria from blood and other normally sterile body sites, resistance to ceftriaxone rose from 8% in 2018 to 72% in 2021 [4]. In 2026 the Ministry of Health reported that only 20% of E. coli found in bloodstream infections could still be treated with it [5].

When the usual drug fails, patients need other antibiotics that cost more, are often given by injection, and may not be stocked at the local facility. For some infections, very few options are left.

Why is it happening here?

There are several reasons, and they feed into each other.

  • Antibiotics are easy to buy. Across studies in Uganda, more than half of people reported treating themselves with medicines bought without a prescription, and antibiotics were among the medicines most often used this way [6].
  • Antibiotics are used for illnesses they cannot treat. Colds, most sore throats and much diarrhoea are caused by viruses or clear up on their own. Antibiotics do nothing for them.
  • Treatment is often a best guess. Few facilities can grow bacteria from a sample and test which drugs work. In Uganda's referral hospitals, more than half of admitted patients were on antibiotics, yet laboratory results guided only 3% of prescriptions [3].
  • Antibiotics are used heavily in animals. Uganda imports large quantities of antibiotics for livestock and poultry [7]. Resistant bacteria and their resistance genes can move between animals, the environment and people.
  • Infections spread. Without safe water, handwashing and good infection control in health facilities, resistant bacteria pass easily from one person to the next.
  • Some medicines are of poor quality. In one fatal case of meningitis at Mulago, the ceftriaxone vial used was later found to contain less than half the dose on its label [8]. Underdosed medicines fail patients and help resistant bacteria survive.

What it means for you and your family

Resistance turns simple infections into serious ones. A urine infection may need a second or third course of treatment. A newborn with an infection may not respond to the first drugs given. Operations and caesarean sections, which rely on antibiotics to prevent infection, become riskier. Families pay more, stay longer in hospital and lose more working days. A study in nine regional referral hospitals estimated the cost to society at about US$4,983 for each patient with a resistant infection, most of it from lost productivity and early death [9].

What you can do

Small choices, made by millions of people, decide how long our antibiotics keep working.

  1. Do not buy antibiotics without seeing a health worker. If you are unwell, get assessed, and tested where possible.
  2. Ask whether you need an antibiotic. It is fine to ask: is this a bacterial infection, and will an antibiotic help?
  3. Take antibiotics exactly as prescribed. Use the dose and the number of days the health worker gives you. If you feel worse or have side effects, go back rather than stopping or switching on your own.
  4. Never share antibiotics or keep leftovers for later. Take unused tablets back to a pharmacy.
  5. Buy medicines only from licensed pharmacies and drug shops. Check that the packaging is intact and the medicine is within its expiry date.
  6. Prevent infections. Wash hands with soap, drink safe water, and make sure children get all their routine vaccines. An infection prevented is an antibiotic not needed.
  7. If you keep animals, involve a veterinarian. Use antibiotics only to treat sick animals, give the full dose, and keep to the withdrawal period before selling milk, eggs or meat.

What health workers and government are doing

Uganda has a national action plan on AMR for 2024/25 to 2028/29. It follows a One Health approach that brings human health, animal health and the environment together [10]. A national network of laboratories now reports resistance data, and in October 2026 the Ministry of Health launched a national Community of Practice to support better antibiotic use in health facilities [5].

Simple tests help too. When community health workers in rural Uganda used a finger-prick test called CRP to decide which children with a cough or breathing illness needed antibiotics, antibiotic use fell from 92% to 71%, and the children did no worse [11].

The bottom line

Antibiotics are a shared resource. Every unnecessary course makes them a little weaker for everyone, including the newborn, the mother after a caesarean and the patient with sepsis who will need them most. Using them only when they are needed, and exactly as prescribed, is something each of us can do.
